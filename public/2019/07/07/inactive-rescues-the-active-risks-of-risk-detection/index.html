Inactive rescues: The active risks of risk detection

July 7, 2019 · dangerousgifts · epistemology · ethics · water

This past month has seen a rash of stories celebrating the application of artificial intelligence to mental health. The coverage suggest that there is something in the zeitgeist, or, perhaps some aggressive public relations work happening behind the scenes. When discussing the publication of my dissertation as a trade book, one piece of advice my editor offered was that I may need to wait until my writing was more descriptive instead of predictive. We have now arrived.

I realized the conversation shifted when the New York Times op-ed columnist David Brooks wrote a naive opinion piece: How Artificial Intelligence Can Save Your Life: The machines know you better than you know yourself.

Seemingly oblivious to all the ways that utopian hopes for new technologies have horribly backfired, Brooks imagines that artificial intelligence will succeed in helping to predict and prevent suicide. He blatantly ignores the challenges that one of the companies he highlights is now facing, even though their problems were covered the week before in his own paper—California Tests a Digital ‘Fire Alarm’ for Mental Distress: The state is teaming up with Silicon Valley to make mental health services more available. Promises abound, and so do potential problems. Crucially, he  also ignores the NYTimes op-ed a month prior on The Empty Promise of Suicide Prevention. Brooks uncritically promotes a dangerous new paradigm of prediction and prevention.

I have written about many of the problems I see with pathalogizing risk, e.g. The Rise of Surveillance Psychiatry and the Mad Underground. The scholar and New York Times columnist Zeynep Turfeki has also written about the risks (many already realized) posed when corporations get their hands on this kind of behavioral data (Think You’re Discreet Online? Think Again), and she has warned us to consider how this data will easily be used to target manipulative advertising, deny us insurance coverage and to discriminate against job applicants. A deeper, less obvious threat is what this data looks like in the hands of the mental health system, and how so-called “digital phenotyping” will change the definition of normal itself and vastly expand the diagnostic net. This is also rapidly underway—better brush up on your grammar if you want to avoid a scizophrenic diagnosis (IC Researchers Seek to Improve Treatment for Schizophrenia Under New $2.7 Million NIMH Grant;  See also: The Coming Computational Approach To Psychiatry).

What could possibly go wrong?  One of the best counterpoints to these initiatives is this excellent investigative story by ProPublica on Aggression Detectors  currently being deployed in schools and hospitals around the country (as covered on Redacted Tonight (17:34): https://youtu.be/gVhU48TYCWs?t=1054).  ProPublica went into schools that purchased and deployed expensive audio capture and analysis systems sold with the promise of preventing the next school shooting. The manufacturer’s claim these microphones would detect aggression, but ProPublica’s tests demonstrate that they have a tendency to mix up laughter and anger and mistake locker doors slamming for gunshots. Tuning them is fraught, and false positives and negatives abound. All sorts of bias are likely, as expressions of emotion are culturally conditioned. The premise is also flawed since school shooters often display quiet rage before attacking, not an audible outbust. The systems also record all audio in the school, and are being used by administrators to crack down on vaping in the bathroom.

The aggression detectors story is important for a few reasons. First, it shows how these systems will be used to regulate all forms of affect, not just depression and anxiety. The tendency for saviors to swoop in and rescue those are suffering is a primary rhetorical technique that is being used to sell the entire paradigm of prediction and prevention if mental health crises to the public. ProPublica’s methods were also rigorous, as they went beyond the company’s brochure and tested the systems for themselves by simulating realistic expressions.  For years I have feared that computational systems would be used to police normal, deviance and dissent; and, that they would be first deployed in schools and prisons. Sucks to be proven right.

A fundamental misunderstanding inherent in so many of these projects reminds me Chris Anderson’s 2008 essay in Wired The End of Theory: The Data Deluge Makes the Scientific Method Obsolete.  Data cannot be interpreted without a theory, even if we don’t recognize that we are implicitly utilizing one in our analysis. Crisis Text Line’s inferences about which keywords indicate who is in crisis presumes that their counselors are calling law enforcement (“active rescues”) appropriately, and not missing opportunities when they should (if ever). Without this critical correction, dispatching emergency services can quickly become rampant, often with horrible consequences (People with Untreated Mental Illness 16 Times More Likely to Be Killed By Law Enforcement). It is widely understood that Americans are over-diagnosed and over-medicated. And, in many cases clinicians cannot even identify metal health conditions effectively (Depression in Black Boys Begins Earlier Than You Think).

With such large gaps and discrepancies in practice, which models will be used to train the algorithms?

So many of these initiatives promise better “management” and “treatment”, though the details of their programs focus on early detection and managing risk. When I talk to crisis counselors they uniformly express that they do not have difficulty recognizing risk—rather, they need better tools for supporting those in crisis, not identifying it.

I’m not a Luddite. I think it is possible to redirect this wizardly technology to help support people better. Doing this well starts with inclusive design—people with lived experience need to be involved in the design and shaping the systems meant to support them. Nothing about us without us.

I also think “that AI/Machine learning thing” could really help improve the training and development of the providers offering support to those in crisis. Instead of focusing the diagnostic lens on the recipients of services, let’s start by developing better tools to help providers improve their skills and experience.  I am imagining just-in-time learning modules that caregivers could query (e.g. skilltree.gsapp.org), or might be suggested to them algorithmically based on an analysis of the interaction. Did that last crisis intervention go well? What could I have done differently if I encounter a similar situation again?

Of course, as with so much else about mental health (and, more broadly, with education) little is known about what works well.  These same systems could be used to gain more insight on successful interventions that have a positive impact. It is critical to have more confidence that an intervention is successful before we start using those data sets to have the machines amplify those approaches and deliver more of the same.  Business as usual is failing. Let’s not amplify our current approaches with super-charged algorithms without reflecting critically on what helps and what harms.
